2WayMirror

Another company. Another initial call.

Thirty to sixty minutes on your background, what you’re looking for, and whether the practical details line up. A hiring manager conversation a week later. Then a technical round.

Across the companies in your pipeline, you give the same answers, tell the same stories, and explain the same projects. Across their candidates, recruiters and hiring managers ask the same questions. Hours of conversation and weeks of scheduling can pass before either side discovers a mismatch.

2WayMirror lets both sides do that groundwork before booking the time. A candidate prepares their answers once and shares a dedicated link with each company, tailored to the kind of role they’re pursuing.

  • Recruiters check background, availability, compensation, and practical fit.
  • Hiring managers explore examples of ownership, collaboration, and difficult decisions.
  • Engineers examine projects, code, and technical tradeoffs as evidence of capability.

The exchange goes both ways. The company answers the candidate’s questions about the team, expectations, and working environment in the same space. Both sides get information they would otherwise spend calls collecting.

That means less repetition, earlier decisions about fit, and fewer meetings spent establishing the basics. When you do meet, you can start with something specific: “I read about the migration you led. We’re facing something similar. Can we talk about the tradeoffs?”

This project is my own example. Below, you can explore its architecture, the choices behind it, and how I used AI to build it—then decide what you’d like to ask me about.

How it works

One CloudFront distribution serves the app and routes /api to the API, so no CORS.

Content is read from a private bucket per session token and never shipped in the JavaScript.

Sessions are rows with an expiry, not deployments. Creating one is a single write.

How it ships

Three decisions

of seven

Always deployed, sessions as data.

Chosen

One deployment that stays up; a session is one row with a token and an expiry.

Instead of

Spinning up infrastructure per recruiter when a link is needed.

Because: A link is ready the moment the reply is written, revoking is a one-field update, and idle cost is effectively zero on pay-per-use services.

One DynamoDB table.

Chosen

A single on-demand table designed from the four access patterns.

Instead of

Postgres on RDS, or SQLite on the Lambda filesystem.

Because: No idle charge, no VPC or connection pooling beside Lambda, and no durable disk was needed. The trade is that new access patterns need design, not a migration.

Private content, public code.

Chosen

Real content in a private repo and bucket, served only through the token-gated API.

Instead of

Bundling content into the JavaScript, or keeping the whole repo private.

Because: The code stays demonstrable, the content stays private, and a content edit is an upload rather than a deploy.

The other four, and the options each one rejected, are in the repo as dated decision records.

How AI was used

I used an AI assistant throughout this project, and I want to be precise about the split. The idea is mine. I directed the architecture and made the final decisions, documenting the reasoning and alternatives in decision records. The assistant wrote the application code, tests, and infrastructure, and critiqued my drafts of the recruiter-facing answers without writing them. I reviewed the changes, challenged the implementation, and decided what to merge. Nothing was merged that I cannot explain in detail.
