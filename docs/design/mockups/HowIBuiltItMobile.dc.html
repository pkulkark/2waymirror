2WayMirror

Another company. Another initial call.

Thirty to sixty minutes on your background, what you are looking for, and whether the practical details line up. A hiring manager conversation a week later. Then a technical round. Across the companies in your pipeline you give the same answers, tell the same stories, and explain the same projects, and the recruiter on the other side asks the same opening questions of candidate after candidate. Both sides invest those hours before discovering what could have been clear much earlier: the compensation does not align, the role is not what the posting said, or the team works in a way the candidate would not choose.

2WayMirror moves that groundwork ahead of the meeting. A candidate prepares their experience once and shares a dedicated link with each company, tailored to the kind of role they are pursuing. The hiring team explores it when it suits them:

  • Recruiters check background, availability, compensation, and practical fit.
  • Hiring managers read concrete examples of ownership, collaboration, and difficult decisions.
  • Engineers examine projects, code, and technical tradeoffs as evidence of capability and a starting point for assessment.

The company answers the candidate's questions in the same space. Both sides spot mismatches earlier, carry context into the next stage, and decide which conversations are worth scheduling. When you do meet, you start further along: "I read about the migration you led. We are facing something similar. Can we talk about the tradeoffs?"

This project is my own example. Below is the story of building 2WayMirror: the implementation, the decisions, and the lessons. Work you can explore before deciding what you would like to ask me about.

How it works

One CloudFront distribution serves the app and routes /api to the API, so no CORS.

Content is read from a private bucket per session token and never shipped in the JavaScript.

Sessions are rows with an expiry, not deployments. Creating one is a single write.

How it ships

Three decisions

of seven

Always deployed, sessions as data.

Chosen

One deployment that stays up; a session is one row with a token and an expiry.

Instead of

Spinning up infrastructure per recruiter when a link is needed.

Because: A link is ready the moment the reply is written, revoking is a one-field update, and idle cost is effectively zero on pay-per-use services.

One DynamoDB table.

Chosen

A single on-demand table designed from the four access patterns.

Instead of

Postgres on RDS, or SQLite on the Lambda filesystem.

Because: No idle charge, no VPC or connection pooling beside Lambda, and no durable disk was needed. The trade is that new access patterns need design, not a migration.

Private content, public code.

Chosen

Real content in a private repo and bucket, served only through the token-gated API.

Instead of

Bundling content into the JavaScript, or keeping the whole repo private.

Because: The code stays demonstrable, the content stays private, and a content edit is an upload rather than a deploy.

The other four, and the options each one rejected, are in the repo as dated decision records.

How AI was used

I built this with an AI assistant in the loop, and I want to be precise about the split. The idea, the architecture, and every decision are mine, written up as decision records with the options I rejected. The assistant wrote scaffolding, tests, and infrastructure from those decisions, and critiqued my drafts of the recruiter-facing answers without writing them. Nothing merged that I cannot explain at a whiteboard.
